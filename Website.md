**Understanding Sleep Study Reports**

**Understanding Common Sleep Study Terms and Parameters**

Sleep studies provide valuable information about breathing patterns, oxygen levels, and other physiological parameters during sleep. Understanding some of the commonly used terms can help individuals become more familiar with the information that may appear in a sleep study report.

The information provided below is intended for educational purposes and explains commonly used sleep study parameters and terminology.

**Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI)**

The Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) is one of the most commonly used parameters in sleep studies. It represents the frequency of breathing interruptions and reductions that occur during sleep.

AHI is widely used in sleep medicine and is commonly referenced when classifying the severity of sleep-disordered breathing.

**Commonly Used AHI Classification**

| AHI | Classification |
| ----- | ----- |
| Less than 5 | Normal |
| 5 to 14.9 | Mild |
| 15 to 29.9 | Moderate |
| 30 or higher | Severe |

**Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI)**

The Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI) reflects the number of times oxygen levels decrease during sleep.

ODI is commonly reviewed alongside other sleep study parameters to better understand oxygen-related changes that may occur during the night.

**Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂)**

Oxygen saturation (SpO₂) represents the percentage of oxygen carried in the blood and is routinely monitored during sleep studies.

Monitoring oxygen saturation during sleep provides information about oxygen levels throughout the recording period.

**Lowest Oxygen Saturation**

Many sleep study reports include the lowest recorded oxygen saturation observed during the study.

This parameter reflects the lowest oxygen level measured during the monitoring period.

**Apnea**

Apnea refers to a temporary pause in breathing during sleep.

Sleep studies may record the number and type of apnea events that occur throughout the night.

**Hypopnea**

Hypopnea refers to a partial reduction in airflow during sleep.

Sleep studies commonly record hypopnea events in addition to apnea events when evaluating breathing patterns.

**Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)**

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) is the most common type of sleep apnea and occurs when the upper airway becomes narrowed or blocked during sleep.

OSA is one of the most frequently evaluated conditions in sleep medicine.

**Central Sleep Apnea (CSA)**

Central Sleep Apnea (CSA) occurs when breathing disturbances are related to reduced respiratory drive from the central nervous system.

CSA differs from OSA in its underlying mechanism.

**Snoring**

Many sleep studies record snoring-related information.

Snoring may occur independently or alongside other sleep-related breathing disorders.

**Body Position During Sleep**

Some sleep studies evaluate body position throughout the night.

Sleeping position may influence breathing patterns in certain individuals.

**Heart Rate During Sleep**

Heart rate monitoring is commonly included in sleep studies and provides information regarding heart rate patterns during sleep.

**Understanding Sleep Study Results**

Sleep studies may contain multiple parameters and measurements that are reviewed collectively by qualified healthcare professionals as part of the overall evaluation process.

Each sleep study is unique, and the significance of individual findings should always be considered within the context of the complete clinical assessment.
